# Benchmark: monitoring deployed risk models on real patient records

Every table on this page is generated by `benchmarks/tables.py` from `results/benchmark_summary.json`, which the *Data and benchmark* workflow produced from the archives recorded in `data/manifest.json` and `data/manifest_sepsis.json`. CI fails if the tables and the results disagree.

## Why a semi-synthetic benchmark

To score a monitor you need the truth: how the deployed model actually performs in each month. A real deployment never reveals it, because once alerts change care, the untreated outcome of treated patients is never observed. The benchmark uses real patients whose care was not influenced by any model, treats their recorded outcome as the untreated outcome, and simulates the deployment on top. Patients, features and outcomes are real. Alerts, treatment decisions and the treatment effect are simulated, with the assumptions below. Each month is drawn with replacement from a known population, so the model's true calibration and discrimination in every month are known exactly.

## Data

| Dataset | Records | Outcome | Source and license |
|---|---|---|---|
| UCI Diabetes 130-US Hospitals, 1999 to 2008 | 69,973 first encounters (of 101,766), after removing deaths and hospice discharges | 30 day readmission, 8.97% | doi:10.24432/C5230J, CC BY 4.0 |
| UCI Sepsis Survival Minimal Clinical Records, Norway 2011 to 2012 | 110,204 admissions (no patient identifier, so repeat admissions remain) | In hospital death, 7.35% | doi:10.24432/C53C8N, CC BY 4.0 |

The diabetes cohort rules follow Strack et al. (2014). The sepsis data has three predictors: age, sex and number of prior sepsis episodes (Chicco and Jurman, 2020). Both datasets are CC BY 4.0 on their UCI pages. The sepsis license statement was read from the page by the workflow and is recorded in its manifest; the diabetes license was recorded when the archive was first added.

## Models

Each dataset is split 30% development (fit the model), 20% reference (pre-deployment validation) and 50% pool (the deployed population). Two models per dataset: logistic regression on standardized features, and histogram gradient boosting with early stopping. Diabetes models use 24 features; sepsis models use the three available.

The models are deliberately ordinary. CounterSignal monitors whatever model is deployed; it does not need a strong one.

## Simulated deployment

* 12 months; 2,000 patients a month for diabetes and 3,000 for sepsis (150 and 200 in the small hospital scenario).
* Patients at or above the reference 90th percentile of risk are alerted.
* The baseline probability of treatment is 70% for alerted and 5% for other patients, with the log odds rising by 0.5 per standard deviation of one recorded factor (prior inpatient stays for diabetes, age for sepsis). Because alerted patients score higher on that factor, about four in five of them are treated (77% to 81% in a check deployment); about 10 to 11% of patients are alerted.
* Treatment prevents 40% of the events it is applied to; the model unchanged scenario of the diabetes logistic configuration is also run at 20% and 60%.
* The CounterSignal treatment model uses the score's log odds, the alert flag and the recorded clinical factors (prior inpatient stays and age for diabetes; age and prior episodes for sepsis). The Feng et al. monitor conditions on the same covariates.
* Acceptable observed/expected range 0.80 to 1.25; alarm limit set for a 5% chance of any false alarm in 12 months.
* 200 deployments per scenario, 200 bootstrap resamples per month, with the treatment model refitted in each resample.

These treatment numbers are assumptions chosen to make the problem visible, not estimates of any real programme.

## Scenarios

| Scenario | What happens |
|---|---|
| Model unchanged | nothing changes; the model's true O/E is 0.98 to 0.99, inside the acceptable range, so no alarm is wanted (the alarm's null of O/E exactly 1 holds only approximately) |
| Case mix | from month 5, patients with more prior inpatient stays (diabetes) or older patients (sepsis) are oversampled; the model is unchanged, but in the new population its true O/E moves slightly (table above) and its true AUC moves by about 0.06 to 0.08, up for diabetes and down for sepsis |
| Drift among alerted patients | from month 5, events among alerted patients become about three times as common (their O/E about 2.5), sized so the model's true O/E in the population rises to 1.35, outside the acceptable range; population AUC rises, so this is concentrated under prediction among alerted patients rather than general deterioration |
| Small hospital | the model is unchanged, with 150 or 200 patients a month |
| Unlogged treatment driver | the model is unchanged, but treatment also depends, at 1.0 log odds per standard deviation, on a factor the monitor's treatment model is not given (prior emergency visits for diabetes, prior sepsis episodes for sepsis). In both datasets that factor is also a risk model input, so the score partly carries it |

## Monitors compared

* **Standard**: predictions against every patient's recorded outcome.
* **Untreated only**: treated patients dropped, no reweighting.
* **Feng et al.**: an independent implementation of the score based CUSUM of Feng et al. (arXiv:2211.09781), run on untreated patients and calibrated on untreated patients drawn under the deployment policy, as the authors' companion code calibrates on standard of care patients; here that is a 6 month run-in before monitoring starts. It tests whether outcomes change relative to that run-in, given the score and the treatment confounders.
* **CounterSignal**: untreated patients weighted by the inverse probability of remaining untreated.

The standard, untreated only and CounterSignal monitors share one alarm rule and one limit and test absolute calibration (O/E = 1). The Feng et al. monitor has its own statistic and limits.

The rise in risk sits among alerted patients, most of whom are treated. The standard monitor sees fewer events than predicted before the change (prevented events) and more after it, so it often alarms early for the wrong reason. The untreated only view sees few of the patients where the change happens. CounterSignal caught the change in at least 99.5% of deployments in every configuration, usually in the second month.

### When the main assumption fails: a treatment driver the monitor cannot see

<!-- tables:alarms_hidden -->
| Dataset, model | Standard | Untreated only | Feng et al. | CounterSignal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diabetes, logistic | 97.0% | 6.5% | 6.5% | 3.0% |
| Diabetes, boosting | 99.0% | 6.0% | 7.0% | 7.5% |
| Sepsis, logistic | 100.0% | 7.0% | 5.0% | 12.0% |
| Sepsis, boosting | 100.0% | 15.0% | 5.0% | 12.0% |
<!-- /tables -->

When clinicians treat on information that is not logged, the weights cannot fully correct for it. CounterSignal's alarm rate rose to 12.0% in both sepsis configurations; untreated only also rose, to 15.0% for sepsis with boosting. In the diabetes configurations it was 3.0% and 7.5%, no higher than with nothing hidden. A plausible reason for the difference, not isolated by the benchmark, is that the sepsis model has only three predictors, so the hidden prior episodes count carries more of the treatment decision. This is the failure the method notes warn about, and the reason the treatment drivers must be logged.

With nothing changing, the untreated only estimate had the lowest error, because untreated patients are most of the population and the models are close to calibrated outside the alerted group. Once risk changes among treated patients, its intervals contain the truth in well under half of months (averaged over all 12, including the four before the change), while CounterSignal's contain it in 90.4% to 93.4%. CounterSignal's 95% intervals are slightly below their nominal coverage in every configuration (90.4% to 94.5%).

### Calibration data matter for the Feng et al. monitor

With nothing changed, the Feng et al. monitor's alarm rate depends on where its calibration data come from. Calibrated on the pre-deployment reference split, whose observed/expected differs from the deployed pool by chance (for diabetes 1.066 and 1.072 against 0.986 and 0.988; for sepsis 1.015 and 1.013 against 0.978 and 0.979), it reads that difference as a change.

## Limits of this benchmark

* Treatment, its effect and its dependence on covariates are simulated, and in all but one scenario every driver of treatment is logged and correctly modelled.
* Months are draws from one fixed pool per dataset, so there is no real calendar drift; changes are introduced by reweighting real records. Patients can recur across and within months.
* Two datasets, two model types and one alert policy.
* The Feng et al. monitor is an independent implementation, not the authors' code, which carries no license. Its parameter estimation correction uses the consistent sign throughout; the companion code applies the opposite sign to the calibration data's term only (see the module docstring).

## Reproduce

```bash
pip install -e .
python benchmarks/run_benchmark.py --config diabetes-logistic --replicates 200 --n-boot 200
python benchmarks/aggregate.py
python benchmarks/tables.py --write
```

Configurations: `diabetes-logistic`, `diabetes-gbm`, `sepsis-logistic`, `sepsis-gbm`. The archives must be in `data/raw/`; the workflow downloads them from UCI and checks them against the manifests. On GitHub hosted runners the simulations for each configuration took between 80 and 117 minutes, excluding setup.
